# Rail Roadmap — 6-Month Horizon

**Authored:** 2026-04-30, after a full session of compiler/runtime/stdlib work.
**Premise:** synthesizes everything seen across today's work — the float-TCO bug discovery, arena substrate redesign, Garmin Pass 6/7 findings, model-side reset, and the broader codebase audit. This document picks a direction.

## What Rail is right now

A 5,944-line self-hosting compiler in `tools/compile.rail`, producing a 940 KB ARM64 binary that compiles itself byte-identically. Zero C-runtime dependencies; ARM64 assembly embedded in the compiler. 137-test suite passes. Stdlib spans tensors, transformers, TLS 1.3, Thumb-2 disassembly, sockets, HTTP — about 23 KLOC, all in Rail. Side projects: a small LM training stack (`tools/train/`), a Garmin watch reverse-engineering project, an LSP stub, an MCP server stub.

The compiler is small, the language is mostly stable, the runtime is auditable. The training stack and the side projects are where most of the work happens day-to-day.

## What today told us

Three things came into focus today that should shape the next 6 months:

### 1. Silent reliability bugs are the dominant failure mode

The float-TCO bug ran for 17 days, invalidated weeks of model results, and was discovered by accident (writing a smoke test for unrelated work). The cost was not the fix (15 minutes) but the time from "introduced" to "discovered" — and during that window, every measurement was confounded.

Three more recent silent-bug classes are documented in memory:
- The `gpu_available 0` re-eval pattern (re-allocates per call due to nullary binding rule) — perf bug, observable but not failure
- The arena_reset chain-drain assumption (turned out to be sound, but only verified empirically today)
- The `_rail_alloc` malloc-fallback path lacking instrumentation until A1.P1 shipped

The pattern: when the reasoning was "this should be fine because X," X was wrong, but nothing screamed. **The next 6 months should bias toward defensive-by-construction over performance.** Run the full test suite as a smoke check; add diagnostic counters before optimizations; require empirical verification of "should be safe" reasoning before committing changes that remove guards.

### 2. The model side has been operating on bad data

Per `MODEL_SESSION_HANDOFF.md`, every CPU-substrate measurement from 2026-04-13 to 2026-04-30 is suspect. The "compile-zero wall" might not be a wall. The "Comprehend ceiling" might be substrate-induced. The "Spur-Fix negative arc" needs re-evaluation. **Until the model team re-benches under the fixed compiler, we don't know what we know.** Treat every model-side conclusion from the last 17 days as "to be verified."

### 3. The arena work has the cleanest infrastructure surface

A1.P1/P4/P5 + RAIL_ARENA_TRACE + envp passthrough produced a well-documented, instrumented allocator with diagnostic counters at `alloc_stats_snapshot` indices 14/15/16. This is the model for how all future runtime systems should ship: counters first, optimization second, code change with a measurement story. The pattern works. Apply it elsewhere.

## Strategic priorities for the next 6 months

Three tiers, ranked by EV under "reliability and usability are the goals."

### Tier 1 — Foundations (do first, weeks 1–4)

**1.1 — Verify everything the model team thought they knew.** Re-bench Spur-0.1, Spur-Fix v0.2/v0.3, v0.7 BEST under the post-fix compiler. Re-run the N=300 Comprehend experiment. Numbers either reproduce (and the conclusions stand) or change (and we have new starting points). Either outcome is high-information. **Estimated time:** 4–8 hours of model-team work. **Blocker for everything else.**

**1.2 — Audit the rest of the stdlib for silent-corruption classes.** The float-TCO bug was one of probably several similar patterns where a guard was removed because "should be fine." Targets: `_rail_str_append` allocation path, `_rail_chained_malloc` direct callers, the `gpu_available 0` re-eval, the conservative GC stack scan boundary at `_rail_stack_top`. For each: write a focused test; verify behavior; add a counter or guard if needed. **Estimated time:** 1 week. **Outcome:** known reliability floor, with regression tests.

**1.3 — Test runner full parallelization.** `quick` shipped today (5.2 sec for 15 tests). Full suite is still 10+ min. Adding `--range A B` flag through the 137 test sites is mechanical (~2 hours) and lets a shell wrapper run 4 parallel processes covering quarters of the range. Target: full suite under 1 minute. **Outcome:** every contributor's iteration loop tightens by 10×.

**1.4 — Source positions through AST (A10).** Two-pass commit: parser emits pos-prefixed nodes; consumers thread positions to error sites. Every future compile error becomes useful instead of cryptic. **Estimated time:** 1–2 days. **Outcome:** the language is approachable.

### Tier 2 — Substrate consolidation (weeks 5–10)

**2.1 — GPU dylib repair.** The MTLBuffer pool reuse hypothesis from `dylib_first_token_only.md` is the last-known-position. Audit `tools/metal/tensor_gpu_lib.m`'s `tgl_*_half_host` wrappers for buffer lifecycle issues. If repaired, GPU inference is unblocked; training throughput could jump back to GPU-rate. **Estimated time:** 4–8 hours of focused Metal debugging. **EV:** very high — affects every future training run's wall-clock time.

**2.2 — Generational collector wired up.** The Cheney semi-spaces (`_rail_young_a/b`, 64 MB each, currently unused static BSS) are scaffolded but not active. Wiring up gives short-lived allocations a fast path that's cheaper than full mark-sweep. A1.P4 set the precedent for migrating to runtime mmap; do that for `_rail_young_*` as part of this. **Estimated time:** 1–2 weeks. **EV:** medium — some workloads benefit; not every run.

**2.3 — `--strict` typecheck flag (A2).** The design is in `docs/strict-typecheck-design.md`. Refactor warnings into a counter pattern; expose `--strict` to halt on first warning. Catches `head` on non-list, arithmetic on string, wrong arity. **Estimated time:** half-day. **EV:** medium-high for new contributors; lower for current developers who know the gotchas.

**2.4 — Per-thread arena (designed in arena-design.md).** Required if Rail ever ships preemptive concurrency or parallel inference. Right now the single-arena assumption is baked into codegen (heap_ptr is global). Designing this NOW (before any concurrency code ships) is cheap; designing it AFTER is a refactor.

### Tier 3 — Strategic bets (weeks 11–24)

**3.1 — Compile-loss-during-training (v0.8).** Already designed in `docs/plans/COMPILE_LOSS_DESIGN.md`. Conditional on Tier 1.1 unblocking the compile-rate floor. If it does — this is the structural advantage Rail has over Python+PyTorch projects (the compiler is in-tree, fast, char-compatible, and ownable). Wiring it into the loss function transforms rerank-time grading into training-time signal. **EV:** very high if Tier 1.1 unlocks it; moderate otherwise.

**3.2 — Backend abstraction (A7).** Currently macOS ARM64 / Linux ARM64 / x86_64 / Thumb-2 (disasm-only) are parallel paths. Refactoring to IR + per-ISA emit table is multi-week work. The unlock is: a real Rail → Thumb-2 backend for Cortex-M targets, useful for Garmin firmware-takeover Phase 2. **EV:** speculative; depends on whether Cortex-M targets become real goals.

**3.3 — LSP server made real (D2).** Stub today. Real LSP — hover, autocomplete, incremental sync, diagnostics — would make Rail approachable to non-experts. Requires a contributor base bigger than the current size to justify. **EV:** low immediately, high if Rail ever ships externally.

**3.4 — Garmin Phase 2 (firmware takeover).** Pass 6/7 mapped the producer-side surface for the watch. Pass 8 (consumer-side: UI render, sync, boot enumeration) would extend that. Beyond Pass 8 is the actual firmware-replacement work, which requires either Cortex-M codegen (3.2 above) or a different toolchain. **EV:** depends on personal interest; not on the critical path for the language itself.

**3.5 — TLS 1.3 P-384 perf (D1).** The `anthropic_client.rail` workaround currently uses unsafe leaf-only certificate verification because full P-384 chain takes ~90 s. Real fix is NAF/Montgomery ladder optimization in `stdlib/x25519.rail` or equivalent. ~D4 crypto perf work. **EV:** medium — closes a real safety hole in the only stdlib client doing outbound TLS.

## What NOT to do

- **Don't add features before Tier 1.1 resolves.** Every measurement we currently have is suspect. Building on suspect measurements compounds the problem.
- **Don't optimize what isn't measured.** A1.P5 set the standard. Every future perf claim should come with a counter that proves it.
- **Don't remove correctness guards without empirical verification.** Today's float-TCO bug was a removed guard. The lesson is durable.
- **Don't take on D5 work (A7 backend abstraction, A8 GC stack maps, D4 Thumb-2 backend) without a specific use case driving it.** They're real but speculative; the current paths work.

## Decision points for the project owner

Three open questions that shape the trajectory:

1. **Is Rail ultimately going public?** If yes, 1.4 (source positions), 2.3 (--strict), and 3.3 (real LSP) become near-term priorities. If no, they stay in the long tail.

2. **Is GPU training the throughput bottleneck the team will sustain investment in?** If yes, Tier 2.1 (dylib repair) is high-priority. If no, the CPU substrate + KV-narrowing is good enough; treat the dylib as a closed chapter.

3. **Does the Spur-class research continue, or does the engineering side become the focus?** The model side has clear next experiments (compile-loss, larger-corpus Spur-Fix), but they're conditional on Tier 1.1 unblocking compile rate. The engineering side has clean targets (1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 2.1, 2.2, 2.3) that don't depend on model results.

These don't need to be answered today, but they should be answered before a long-arc commitment to any of the Tier 2/3 items.
